> **Note:** This article is obviously about the struggles with serious mental illness on a widespread scale today, and it mentions many extreme
> narratives that developed around that—things like online bullying, romanticization, clout-chasing, and all that. Don't read this if it'll ruin
> your day!

---

Many many articles discuss the mental health crisis happening right now amongst today's teens and young people. Why did we all get so anxious and depressed? Was it the internet? The older gens? Social media algorithms? What? Etc? Plenty of bullshit nonsense gets pushed to explain it of course and we all know it's *NONSENSE*. The reality of it is far more ineffable. Mental health struggles usually are.

Anyways. Instead of any of that though I'd like to discuss how we see our own mental health right now. How does the media portray the burgeoning epidemic of troubled teens? How do we ourselves as younger people experiencing those things express it? Like I said there's plenty written about the how and why of this malaise. The narratives of it all though tend to be silently controlling at times like rare costumes donned willingly or forced upon us by our parents or peers. This topic is quite the third rail by comparison actually. Surprising. You'd think people would care about how we (and others) see ourselves and talk about it contextualizing things? Maybe find it significant?

The truth is there is an ugly cornucopia of narratives available for our struggles. They can be hard to escape too. These are often religious grotesquely medicalized sickeningly aestheticized adversarial in nature or monetized. They are rarely sensible accurate humane or patient-led in a gentle understanding way. Narratives for whatever reason have turned deeply important for my generation so…

---

To begin I believe that the media portrayal of mental health struggles never really helps anything. Many movies TV shows TikToks YouTube videos and influencers try to be sensitive towards these topics (while also cashing in on them). They simply aren't and give the wrong ideas. That kind of media changes how people see mental health overall right now and in a bad way. I'm terribly terribly terribly sorry if any of those "crazy girl self-actualizes through trauma" edits or videos are your favorite. Things can be entertaining without being accurate useful or giving good vibes right?

I also ~~(perhaps more controversially)~~ believe that we often express our struggles with mental health in strange and novel ways. Sometimes this is because of the influence of popular media about mental illness I'm sure. There are other reasons as well such as lack of adult intervention and some other unfortunate generational factors. It could've been avoided with the aforementioned adult guidance but that doesn't happen much. It did for me but seemed very very very uncommon.

These narratives of mental illness featuring young people today serve a limited number of purposes in different genres. In a lot of cases they are about salvation in some sense of the term. In America, and big parts of Europe this is often a literal religious phenomena where the mental illness ends or is ameliorated by forming a religious "relationship with Christ" or something of the sort. As a teen experiencing depression for the first time this was the first thing people thought to "try" on me. While none of my peers especially assumed my mental health issues were because I wasn't a Christian at the time. Maybe it's easy for a normal person who hasn't dealt with mental illness to assume this is how it works. Uhm no. When I came to Christ it was of my own accord. I don't find this kind of manipulative approach to religious choices appropriate. Ever.

I suspect things like those viral testimony videos and other narratives where someone becomes a Christian and suddenly gets cured of any mental illness inspire a lot of deep problems. A lot of them imply demons (yes literal demons) are to blame for mental illness. Thus becoming a Christian banishes them. Now that I'm a Christian later in life I know this is nonsense and that Christians can have things like depression tics anxiety etc too. Some "evangelical" types really really want to believe this narrative though. This is partially just because it lets them play saviors themselves by convincing others to "become Christians." It's also because it lets them exteriorize mental illness and blame something (the demons) rather than admitting the issue is really complex sometimes and you might need to be trauma-informed to approach it. I can't imagine finding this helpful. I can't imagine finding this helpful.

Did you think the religious attempts to understand us stopped there though with the Christians? Nope! Let's not forget the gorgeous and shiny New Age movement which is still trucking along right through today's feeds on its way (allegedly) to whatever the next big shift is. They are well and ready to convince as many of us as possible that we are powerful Indigo Children (or their updated versions) ready to transform the world with our psychic abilities! These New Age Gurus (or whoever they are channeling) deeply want to convince us we are old souls and specially incarnated to save the world. That they claim is the reason for any sense of displacement or unease. Yeah no. I try to be understanding of different spiritualities but… The concept of Indigo Crystal Rainbow or other shiny glow-ish Children really just dovetails too neatly with things like depression trauma anxiety and especially just general neurodivergence to ignore. Ditto for the later "diet" version of this we see with the "I'm an Empath!" movement and all the spiritual TikTok trends rebranding those.

Conversely these salvation narratives also take a secular approach sometimes. In these the person who saves the mentally ill person isn't from a church. They're a doctor a therapist or a parent or even a super-understanding online mutual. In those the narrative is about the "Power of Love" or something similar more like traditional fiction. The patient might be rescued by a particular form of therapy perspective or something else even. It varies. Now if you're struggling with mental illness you should go to a doctor or therapist yes but these narratives are… a little off? They're still comparable story formulae and show up again and again today. And earlier. These are almost invariably written or produced by the therapist/doctor/parent/influencer/etc not the patient even though they typically try to write from the patient's perspective. By try I mean they'll include long passages of details the therapist couldn't have known or seen dwelling on the patient's supposed inner monologue etc. To me I'm 100% fine (and even interested at times) in reading therapists write about (consenting) patients but they shouldn't do so with such rampant disingenuity. All this screams that the book/video/thread is maybe just fiction ghostwritten or both… Either way most of these weave a nice story about… salvation yes.

---

In other situations the stories are about pitting people with one form of mental illness against another as if we were Pokémon or something. Common in movies series and especially comment sections these plots are extremely covert. The protagonists in these could be anyone but the villains have to be Cluster B of some sort. Remember! It is still problematic to villainize most mental illnesses but Cluster B is exempt. Some stories just need a villain to hold things together. Cluster B itself as a diagnostic category runs a strange gamut today. It's always been vilified to a degree. While you cannot diagnose teens with personality disorders people (including authority figures and randos online) do seem to try going by my peers experiences…

Speaking of my peers (and me I guess) let's turn to a discussion of how we portray our own mental health struggles primarily online. I myself (as a younger person anyways) never really posted much about any mental health struggles I was having. I feared how that might look after all. I did chat with other people about them occasionally tho and saw that portrayals really run the gamut as does sheer misinformation. The misinformation is probably the worst part.

I knew people convinced that if they didn't manage their symptoms perfectly they'd spiral into the worst versions of whatever disorder the algorithm told them they had. The idea that certain things cause certain conditions has long been a creepy lie told in various corners. While most of us ended up with the vague idea that a lot of it was bullshit I saw people struggle with relatives and online strangers about it. We weren't educated enough about the reality to defend against it in many cases. I also knew plenty of people who did not grasp that SSRIs take time to work do not "get you high" and are not "happy pills" too. This probably contributes to the pushback against mental healthcare services in our age group. *(They're still fucking terrible tho)* Like come on most people don't want to use something they don't at least tacitly understand right?

---

And… let's walk on unstable ground a bit shall we? Maybe bring up some things people rarely want to acknowledge about us when we are younger? We especially as teens romanticize mental illnesses especially depression to an extreme degree especially in online subcultures. This isn't healthy and causes a multitude of problems. A lot of this romantic aura comes from those above narratives and filters into the mindset but still it is there regardless of who started it out.

The (parody yes stop it it was a parody I'm fairly sure) stuff and all the edgy memes make a lot of us laugh and still do. It's one of the funniest things to come out of today's internet at least. Even today I sometimes wistfully remember sitting with someone whispering a line from something like that and everyone cracking up. It's aggressively current ain't it? These make a little more sense if you realize that we are romanticizing mental illness at that point often to an intensely creepy degree that could be parodied like that. To me it comes across as (probably) a teen writer parodying a very real phenomenon we are frequently coming across and grappling with live online (or off)!

My point? People wouldn't create and spread things like the above passages (and swap them around and turn them into an unprecedented phenomenon) if they didn't claw into something we are already doing just a little bit. And we are. Some of us. Not *intentionally* and certainly not to that caliber but the vibe is there at times so to speak. I'm not talking about something that ostentatious obviously but… yes.

One of my first experiences with shared spaces online was with someone who one day did (as far as I know) make a suicide gesture after letting everyone know about it. It was quite an awful afternoon with folks trying to figure out what to do genuine panic etc… After that she immediately began typing up a memoir-like thread describing the experience intent on posting it. Even at the time as a younger teenager I wasn't sure how to feel about this? On one hand it felt like she wasn't taking what had ostensibly went down seriously and was using it for content production. On the other it had happened to her and wasn't it her right to express as she pleased? To process as she wanted? I didn't know how to view these kinds of posts which are often long and florid. And anyways can I be that critical? I've written bits about my own struggles… I think I did it long after the fact and in a way that didn't terrify or exploit things though? I'm still not sure how to feel about that kind of content!

Most people who experienced mental health struggles didn't dive into that kind of thing. Granted it wasn't uncommon but it wasn't the majority. It happened enough to influence the default culture in disturbing ways. I was adjacent to the "online scene" and saw some of the "caught on camera" or live mental health crises. Those were quite a splash and probably made the rounds given how fast things spread. I remember the drama channels and gossip accounts about it all.

It "called out" people (supposedly) just weaponizing mental health issues for online clout and attention occasionally. Articles and threads would mostly just claim they were "faking it." Usually the more popular ones targeted rather than smaller accounts likely because folks needed the creators around and didn't care about smaller ones. This also happened in the fandom communities I was in as well as in the political spaces occult servers and even some tech discords I was still on. The "faking it" accusation wasn't limited to threats of ending one's life either. "He's not really got [insert diagnosis]" was a common sentiment as was claims of faking any kind of physical illness. We'll ignore the latter because while loathsome it's not the topic here.

---

*Men* in particular got accused of faking that. Whereas women were often accused of faking mental illness "for attention" a man was typically said to be faking it "because he's simply lazy" and wants to avoid adult responsibilities presumably. It doesn't wash either way because both of those things merely complicate one's life further right? If you're truly that lazy you surely won't want to deal with "acting" it and getting diagnosed for example. A lot of the latter accusations towards guys primed the pump (so to speak) for later confusing sentiments targeting young guys. Weirdly? The "faking it" whines were always accompanied by some big speech about how the person was super-offended by the dude because they themselves knew mental illness was super-serious. It was a strange performance that probably continues in some spaces to this day.

It really comes down to us having a really bizarre picture of what mental illness even involves. Is it some kind of demonic harassment fixable if only we'd understand Christ Jesus? Could we be rescued if only we trusted in one special adult who understood things and had read the right books or watched the right videos? Or were we just fine only a bit magical special psychic even. Indigo? What are we supposed to do when our struggles don't fit these weird cinematic or algorithmic narratives? Know how to seek help? How to understand what is happening?

I also personally think some people tried to make their experiences match as a way of seeking validation for what they were feeling. After all if yours didn't were they even real or were you pretending for clout? Do you see a scary loop here? I do. Very strange very toxic and could've been avoided if the schools had actually taught us significantly more about these kinds of things. They had time for other assemblies but not to teach kids about trauma and depression? How to take care of ourselves?

The legacy of this continues to this day with the downright abysmal track record we have with mental health and the many many many grifts out there trying to take advantage of that. I'm sorry if you're a fellow teen reading this and that offends but it's the truth. My generation has a poor track record with these things and we don't exactly have the time to turn things around completely. I feel like I've been supremely lucky compared to most in that my issues were so violently physical that it was difficult to dismiss or romanticize away and generally just involve physical treatments but...

Nowadays though all these narratives have been packaged neatly by influencers who continue to hawk them in various ways to us and our kin. Were any of them ever helpful? Considering how quickly "KYS" became a ~~super-casual~~ insult amongst my age group and younger I can't say we ever got the right idea about this kind of thing.
